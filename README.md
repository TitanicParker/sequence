## The core case

The strongest proposition supported by the record is not that peripheral neuropathy was negligently diagnosed, that alcohol was maliciously blamed, or that every historical episode of foot pain was dystonia.

It is narrower and harder to answer:

**A clinician-generated and expressly unresolved neuropathy hypothesis entered the prospective record in 2017; over time, the uncertainty attached to whether that classification was valid ceased to be visible, while the classification itself remained available to organise treatment and explanation; and when the patient eventually challenged the legitimacy of that classification, the continuity author answered from within it rather than independently establishing the proposition being challenged.**

That proposition can be tested directly from the documents.

### 1. The patient did not arrive with peripheral neuropathy as an established diagnosis

The June 2017 GP referral presented an integrated and causally open problem involving abnormal gait, bilateral foot problems, increased tone, inability to relax, toe clawing, deterioration in dexterity and function, and the explicit question “?parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis.”

The hospital then independently observed a striking lower-limb abnormality:

> “footdrop appearance somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”

Thus, before the neuropathy proposition was introduced, Neurology already possessed both the longstanding foot/lower-limb history and direct evidence of a dystonic-looking neurological phenomenon.

### 2. Peripheral neuropathy entered through the clinician's own inferential act

At synthesis, the clinician wrote that the residual leg/foot symptoms:

> “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy…”

possibly related to previous alcohol intake.

The same passage retained uncertainty: “may have been,” “perhaps,” and “Not clear.”

This is critical.

Peripheral neuropathy was therefore not recorded there as an established finding. It was a **clinician-generated explanatory hypothesis**.

The supplied 2017 material does not show an equivalent evidential bridge establishing peripheral neuropathy or demonstrating why the dystonic-looking lower-limb finding should be separated from the painful/foot/toe territory.

That does not prove neuropathy was impossible.

It establishes something narrower:

**the evidential status of neuropathy at the point of creation was provisional.**

### 3. The provisional synthesis nevertheless became prospective

The uncertainty did not remain merely an isolated speculative sentence.

The discharge architecture incorporated the preceding material into ongoing management through:

> “Plan/Recommendations: As per above.”

The significance is not that these words make every preceding hypothesis a diagnosis. They do something more basic: they make the preceding synthesis part of the prospective documentary state from which subsequent care proceeds.

Accordingly, the system moved forward carrying both:

* well-supported Parkinsonism; and
* an unresolved separate/peripheral explanatory compartment for the residual foot/lower-limb problem.

That second compartment had not been independently established merely because it had become usable.

### 4. The later absence of the words “peripheral neuropathy” does not establish abandonment of the category

This point must be stated carefully.

It would be incorrect to argue that peripheral neuropathy was explicitly written as the active diagnosis in every intervening note.

It was not.

But that is not the relevant test.

The question is whether the continuing painful foot/lower-limb problem remained capable of occupying a separate or neuropathic explanatory compartment while neurological phenomena were dealt with separately.

The later sequence demonstrates exactly that possibility. Parkinsonian foot phenomena, OFF dystonia, toe curling and treatment-state changes could be recognised locally while painful foot symptoms remained available for neuropathic interpretation. The system therefore did not need repeatedly to write “peripheral neuropathy” in order for the original partition to remain operational.

### 5. Later evidence repeatedly pressured the boundary without requiring its historical reconstruction

The record subsequently contains increasingly important neurological evidence in the same anatomical territory.

Within months of discharge, medication withdrawal was reported to affect the feet and gait:

> “my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart.”

Later, a recurrent foot-drop-like phenomenon was explicitly classified as Parkinsonian.

Later still, OFF dystonia, toe curling, medication-state dependence and dopaminergic relief entered the clinical reasoning.

None of these observations proves that all foot pain was dystonic.

Their forensic importance is different:

**they repeatedly created reasons to test the relationship between the neurological and painful foot phenomena.**

The record instead became capable of accommodating multiple local truths simultaneously: structural feet, Parkinsonian movement, dystonia, neuropathic pain and Parkinsonian modulation of pain.

That can be clinically sophisticated and still leave the historical classification event unexamined.

### 6. The complaint response exposes the decisive epistemic transformation

By 2025 the patient expressly challenged the historical reasoning and requested examination of how the clinical model had developed.

The continuity author's response refers to pregabalin as having been introduced for:

> “the neuropathic pain (of whatever cause)…”

while acknowledging that Sinemet CR had produced profound relief.

This formulation matters because it locates the uncertainty differently from 2017.

In 2017:

**Is this peripheral neuropathy? Perhaps. Not clear.**

By the complaint response:

**The pain is neuropathic; its underlying cause may be uncertain.**

Those propositions are not epistemically equivalent.

The first questions the classification.

The second retains the classification and questions its aetiology.

### 7. The record supplied does not show the evidential event that justified that promotion

This is the central evidential challenge.

Between those two states, what independently established that the patient's continuing pain should properly be classified as neuropathic?

Burning pain does not itself establish peripheral neuropathy.

Pregabalin treatment does not establish peripheral neuropathy.

Pregabalin response does not establish peripheral neuropathy.

A possible neuropathy recorded in an earlier letter does not establish peripheral neuropathy.

Repeating “neuropathic pain” does not independently establish the proposition from which that terminology descended.

Real alcohol exposure can justify considering neuropathy, but it does not itself establish that this particular pain was produced by peripheral nerve disease.

And genuine structural foot pathology can coexist with neurological disease without proving that the residual pain belongs outside the neurological phenotype.

Therefore the challenge is not:

**“Prove neuropathy was impossible.”**

It is:

**“Identify the independent evidential step by which your own 2017 ‘perhaps / Not clear’ proposition acquired sufficient status to become the premise of your 2025 explanation.”**

On the supplied record, that step is not demonstrated.

### 8. The alcohol issue makes the transformation more, not less, visible

Alcohol was initially one possible causal explanation attached to the proposed neuropathy.

By the later period, the clinician can retreat from a strong alcohol-causation proposition. That is entirely legitimate.

But abandoning or weakening the proposed **cause** does not necessarily reopen the underlying **classification**.

The structure can therefore change from:

**possible neuropathy ← possibly alcohol-related**

to:

**neuropathic pain ← cause uncertain**

without ever returning to the logically prior question:

**Was the pain correctly placed in the neuropathic category at all?**

That is the crucial distinction.

The clinician can appear increasingly cautious about causation while simultaneously becoming more settled about classification.

### 9. The continuity of authorship removes an important innocent explanation

If a later clinician had simply encountered “neuropathic pain” in an inherited chart, loss of provenance could plausibly explain the change in evidential status.

That explanation is materially weaker here.

The complaint response comes from the continuity author who had participated in creating the original 2017 formulation.

He therefore had direct documentary access to the fact that the original proposition had been expressed as:

* “may have been”;
* “perhaps”;
* and “Not clear.”

This does **not** prove dishonest intention, conscious deception, or an intention to harm.

Those propositions are neither necessary nor established.

What can be said more securely is:

**the 2025 response is a deliberate authored explanation. Within that explanation, the author chooses to preserve the neuropathic classification while locating uncertainty in its cause rather than visibly reopening the validity of the classification itself.**

That is an observable documentary act. No speculation about internal motive is required.

### 10. The complaint therefore creates the strongest possible test — and the response does not visibly perform it

The complaint created an opportunity to ask:

* Where exactly did the neuropathy proposition originate?
* What evidence supported it?
* What was observation and what was inference?
* Was peripheral neuropathy ever independently established?
* How was the 2017 dystonic-looking lower-limb sign reconciled with it?
* What should later Parkinsonian foot phenomena have done to the original partition?
* Did later dopaminergic responsiveness require historical reconsideration?
* Was the continuing pain ever reclassified from first principles?

Instead, the response substantially explains downstream treatment: pregabalin, levodopa caution, pain amplification, dyskinesia risk and eventual Sinemet benefit.

Those may all be legitimate clinical considerations.

But they answer a different question.

The mechanism map captures the distinction precisely: downstream management can be explained extensively while the upstream classification event remains untouched.

### 11. This is why downstream consistency cannot validate the upstream premise

Once the neuropathic category exists, several later events can occur inside it:

neuropathic terminology
→ pregabalin treatment
→ apparent pregabalin response
→ neuropathy-oriented investigation
→ later description of treatment as treatment for neuropathic pain.

But those later events are not necessarily independent evidence for the proposition that began the chain.

If they arose partly because the original category existed, they cannot simply be counted backward as proof that the original category was justified.

That would invert provenance:

**hypothesis → management generated by hypothesis → management history cited as evidence for hypothesis.**

That is the self-reinforcing mechanism that an independent review must break.

### 12. The later PRKN result sharpens the question without answering it retrospectively

The later genetic result confirms that Parkinsonism **and dystonia** are manifestations of the patient's PRKN disease.

That does not prove every painful foot symptom was dystonia.

It does something more disciplined and more important.

It confirms that the category Neurology had already observed in the lower limb in 2017—dystonia—belonged to the patient's actual disease.

Therefore the disappearance of that contemporaneous neurological possibility from the residual 2017 causal synthesis cannot now be dismissed as an irrelevant descriptive curiosity.

It becomes a historical question requiring an answer.

## The hardened proposition

The strongest surviving case is therefore:

**The record does not demonstrate that peripheral neuropathy was ever established as the explanation for the patient's longstanding foot pain. It demonstrates that peripheral neuropathy entered in 2017 as the continuity clinician's expressly uncertain hypothesis; that the surrounding synthesis became part of the prospective record; that later neurological evidence in the same territory was repeatedly capable of being accommodated without reconstructing that original allocation; and that, when the patient finally challenged the allocation itself, the same author answered from the premise of “neuropathic pain” while relocating uncertainty to its cause. The supplied record does not show the independent evidential step that converted the original “perhaps / Not clear” classification into that later premise.**

And the single hardest question is:

**What evidence, independent of the clinician's own 2017 hypothesis and the downstream history generated within it, established that this patient's continuing foot pain was neuropathic in the first place?**

Until that question is answered from independent evidence, **“of whatever cause” does not resolve the complaint. It demonstrates the problem: the cause has been reopened while the clinician-created classification that logically precedes it has not.**
